### MODULE 1 QUIZ QUESTIONS & ANSWER KEY

**Question 1 (Beginner - Knowledge)**
**Category**: Policy Document Recognition
**Difficulty Score**: 1/5

**Question Text**:
> Which of the following is NOT typically found in a standard property insurance policy document?

**Options**:
- A) Policy number and policyholder details
- B) Coverage limits and excess amounts
- C) Historical house sale prices from the past 10 years
- D) Exclusions and conditions

**Correct Answer**: C
**Explanation**: Historical house sale prices are not relevant to insurance policy coverage or terms. Policy documents always include policy numbers, policyholder details (A), coverage limits with excess amounts (B), and exclusions/conditions (D). This is critical information contractors need to identify when reviewing policies.

**Skill Assessed**: Ability to recognize valid policy components
**Remediation Tip**: Review the "Policy Document Overview" section of Module 1 video if incorrect

**Question 2 (Beginner - Knowledge)**
**Category**: Policy Types Identification
**Difficulty Score**: 1/5

**Question Text**:
> A homeowner has damage to their roof and internal damage to cabinets and furniture. Which two policy types would typically apply to this claim?

**Options**:
- A) Building insurance only
- B) Contents insurance only
- C) Building insurance (roof) and Contents insurance (furniture/cabinets)
- D) A single "Home Complete" policy that covers both equally

**Correct Answer**: C
**Explanation**: Building insurance covers the structure (roof, walls, fixed items like built-in cabinets). Contents insurance covers personal belongings and furniture. This is a critical distinction contractors must understand for accurate communication with clients. Note: Some policies combine these, but they are legally separate coverage types.

**Skill Assessed**: Ability to differentiate building vs contents coverage
**Remediation Tip**: Review "Building vs Contents vs Commercial" section if incorrect

**Question 3 (Beginner - Knowledge)**
**Category**: Mandatory vs Optional Coverage
**Difficulty Score**: 2/5

**Question Text**:
> A client mentions their flood damage was not covered by their standard building insurance. Why might this be the case?

**Options**:
- A) All building policies include flood cover—the client is mistaken
- B) Flood cover is optional and must be added to most policies (except Allianz/AAMI which include it)
- C) Contractors cannot discuss flood coverage, only insurers can
- D) The damage was actually storm damage, which IS covered, and they're confused

**Correct Answer**: B
**Explanation**: **CRITICAL KNOWLEDGE**: Most Australian insurers offer flood as OPTIONAL add-on coverage, except Allianz and AAMI which include it as standard. This is one of the most important gaps contractors encounter. Contractors should NOT assume flood is covered—always verify.

**Skill Assessed**: Recognition of mandatory vs optional coverage gaps
**Remediation Tip**: Review "Mandatory vs Optional Coverage" section—this is critical for professional communication

**Question 4 (Beginner - Comprehension)**
**Category**: Policy Language & Terminology
**Difficulty Score**: 2/5

**Question Text**:
> A client says their policy has a "$1,500 excess." What does this mean in practical terms?

**Options**:
- A) They will receive $1,500 extra after a claim is approved
- B) The insurer will pay up to $1,500 per claim
- C) They must pay the first $1,500 of any claim; the insurer covers the rest
- D) The policy can be renewed once every $1,500 payments

**Correct Answer**: C
**Explanation**: The excess (deductible) is the amount the policyholder must pay towards a claim before the insurer covers the remaining amount. This is fundamental to helping clients understand whether to claim. A $1,500 excess on a $2,000 damage means the client pays $1,500, insurer pays $500. This is critical for contractors to explain professionally.

**Skill Assessed**: Understanding of policy terminology
**Remediation Tip**: Review "Policy Language and Terminology" subsection if incorrect

**Question 5 (Intermediate - Application)**
**Category**: Identifying Coverage Gaps
**Difficulty Score**: 3/5

**Question Text**:
> You're reviewing a client's policy and see it covers "Storm, storm surge, and tsunami damage" but lists "Flood damage - EXCLUDED." Which statement is MOST accurate?

**Options**:
- A) The client cannot claim for any water damage whatsoever
- B) Storm damage from heavy rain IS covered, but rising river water/storm surge flooding would NOT be covered
- C) Both storm and flood damage are covered identically
- D) This is a broken policy and the client should contact their insurer immediately to complain

**Correct Answer**: B
**Explanation**: This is a nuanced but critical distinction. "Storm" includes heavy rain damage causing runoff and water entry. However, "flood" (slowly rising water, river overflow, storm surge) is specifically excluded. Contractors often see clients confused about this—they assume "storm" covers all water damage. Understanding this distinction prevents miscommunication during the three-way call with the insurer.

**Skill Assessed**: Nuanced understanding of coverage distinctions

> SCENARIO: During a three-way call, the adjuster mentions the policy has a "20% deductible for water damage" but the client's written policy shows a "$2,000 excess." The client looks confused. What is happening here?

- A) "Sure, I'll secretly bill the insurer and you pay me for the rest"
- B) "I understand your frustration, but I cannot bill the insurer for non-covered work. That would be insurance fraud. - C) "That's not my problem—go ask the insurer"

**Question Text**:
> What does Public Liability Insurance cover for contractors?

**Options**:
- A) Damage the contractor causes to third-party property or injury to third parties
- B) Damage to the contractor's own tools and equipment only
- C) The contractor's personal medical expenses
- D) Legal fees for any lawsuit, regardless of merit

**Correct Answer**: A
**Explanation**: Public Liability protects contractors when THEY cause damage to client property or injure third parties. Example: Contractor's ladder damages a neighbor's fence = Public Liability covers it. This is critical for disaster recovery work where contractors are in damaged properties with exposed hazards.
