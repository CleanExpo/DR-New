**[0:45-3:00] WHAT CONTRACTORS CAN DISCUSS**

**VISUAL**: Three-column layout showing different scenarios with checkmarks

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "Contractors CAN professionally discuss and explain:
>
> **Number One: Policy Document Basics**
> You can explain what's in their policy—the policy number, the coverage limits, the excess amount. You're helping them understand their own document.
>
> Example: 'I see your policy has a $1,500 excess. That means you'll pay the first $1,500 of any claim, and the insurance pays the rest.'
>
> **Number Two: What You Need For the Claim**
> You can explain what documentation you need to support the claim. This is your expertise.
>
> Example: 'I'll need photos from multiple angles, a detailed itemized quote, and proof of ownership for the items being claimed.'
>
> **Number Three: What Your Repair Will Cost**
> You can provide quotes and explain what's included in your scope of work. This is your professional responsibility.
>
> Example: 'My quote includes labor, materials, and a warranty. It doesn't include painting the entire room—just the water-damaged section.'
>
> **Number Four: Explaining Coverage Distinctions (Carefully)**
> You can explain the difference between Building and Contents, or why a specific damage type might fall into a coverage category.
>
> Example: 'Your built-in kitchen cabinets are part of the structure, so they're covered by Building Insurance. But your personal dishes inside them would be Contents.'
>
> These are factual explanations, not giving insurance advice."

**[VISUAL TRANSITION]**: Green checkmark appears next to each scenario, with professional language examples on screen

---

**[3:00-4:30] WHAT CONTRACTORS CANNOT ADVISE ON**

**VISUAL**: Red X symbols appearing next to prohibited scenarios

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "Now, what should you AVOID discussing:
>
> **Number One: Coverage Decisions**
> You CANNOT tell a client whether they're covered or not. That's the insurer's decision.
>
> ❌ DON'T SAY: 'You're definitely covered for this.'
> ✅ DO SAY: 'That's a great question for the insurer. I'll support the claim with documentation, and they'll make the coverage decision.'
>
> **Number Two: Premium or Claims History Impact**
> You're not a financial advisor. Don't speculate about how a claim affects their premiums.
>
> ❌ DON'T SAY: 'This claim will cost you $2,000 more per year in premiums.'
> ✅ DO SAY: 'I'd recommend discussing premium impact with your insurer.'
>
> **Number Three: Policy Changes or Recommendations**
> You might think a client needs additional coverage, but that's between them and their insurer.
>
> ❌ DON'T SAY: 'You should have gotten accidental damage cover.'
> ✅ DO SAY: 'If you want to discuss your coverage options, that's a conversation for your insurer.'
>
> **Number Four: Promises About Outcomes**
> Never promise coverage, approval, or specific outcomes.
>
> ❌ DON'T SAY: 'Don't worry, this will definitely get approved.'
> ✅ DO SAY: 'I'll support the claim with the best documentation possible. The insurer will make the final decision.'"

**[0:45-2:30] STANDARD QUOTE STRUCTURE**

**VISUAL**: On-screen quote template with sections highlighted

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "Every professional quote should have these sections:
>
> **Header Section**:
> - Your company name, license number, contact information
> - Date of quote
> - Client name and address
> - Property address (if different from client address)
> - Quote number (for tracking)
>
> **Item Description Section** (this is critical):
> List each repair item with:
> - Item number
> - Description of work
> - Location (which room/area)
> - Unit price
> - Quantity
> - Line total
>
> **Materials vs. Labor Separation** (this is KEY):
> Keep materials and labor SEPARATE.
>
> ❌ DON'T DO: 'Bathroom repair: $3,500'
> ✅ DO THIS:
> - Remove damaged drywall: $800 (labor)
> - Supply drywall materials: $400 (materials)
> - Paint & finish: $500 (labor + materials)
>
> Separating materials from labor shows transparency and lets the insurer evaluate each component.
>

**[0:45-1:45] WHAT IS SCOPE OF WORK**

**VISUAL**: Definition box on screen with examples expanding

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "A Scope of Work is a written description of exactly what work will be performed, what won't be included, and under what conditions.
>

>

>
> A good scope leaves no room for misunderstanding.
>
> Why is this important for insurance claims?
> - Insurer knows exactly what was approved
> - If work changes mid-project, everyone knows it's a change
> - Client expectations are set clearly
> - Prevents disputes about what was included"

**[0:00-1:00] INTRODUCTION**

---

**[1:00-2:30] WHY EXPECTATIONS MATTER**

**VISUAL**: Animated progression showing how unclear expectations lead to conflict

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "Here's the psychology: When reality meets expectations, people either feel satisfied or disappointed. The gap between expected and actual determines satisfaction.
>
> **High expectations + Great outcome = Satisfied**
> **High expectations + Okay outcome = Disappointed**
> **Realistic expectations + Okay outcome = Satisfied**
>
> As a contractor, you want your clients in that last category. Realistic expectations with realistic outcomes.
>
> This is especially important in insurance claims because many outcomes ARE negotiated and sometimes reduced. If the client expects $10,000 and gets $7,000, they're disappointed. But if you'd said 'The insurer will likely approve 60-80% of requested work,' then $7,000 is within expected range."

---

**[2:30-5:00] SETTING EXPECTATIONS EARLY**

**VISUAL**: Email templates and conversation guides appearing on screen

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "Here's what you should communicate EARLY in the process (Day 1-2, before the three-way call):
>
> **About the Timeline**:
> 'The insurance claim process typically takes 4-8 weeks. The insurer has 10 business days to respond to our initial submission, then they'll assess and approve. During this time, we'll provide weekly updates.'
>
> **About the Approval Process**:
> 'The insurer will review our documentation and decide what's covered. They may approve everything, some items, or ask for additional information. This is normal. We'll support the claim with the best documentation possible.'
>
> **About Potential Outcomes**:
> 'The insurer may approve the full amount, a reduced amount, or ask for modifications to the scope. Our job is to provide professional documentation so they can make an informed decision.'
>
> **About Your Role**:
> 'I'll provide professional documentation and support the claim. I'm not an insurance advisor—my expertise is in the repair. Coverage decisions are the insurer's responsibility.'
>
> These statements are honest, realistic, and prevent the client from expecting guarantees you can't provide."

---

**[5:00-7:30] INTERIM COMMUNICATION**

**VISUAL**: Timeline graphic showing week-by-week updates

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "Between the initial claim and approval, communicate regularly. Why? Because silence creates anxiety, and anxiety creates unrealistic expectations.
>
> **Week 1**: 'I've submitted our documentation. The insurer should respond by [date].'
>
> **Week 2**: 'Still waiting for the insurer's response. This is normal. I'll follow up if we don't hear by [date].'
>
> **Week 3**: 'Got feedback from the insurer—they've approved [amount] and are asking about [specific item]. I'm responding with [additional information].'
>
> **Week 4**: 'Approval received! Here's what's been approved: [specific items]. We can begin work on [date].'
>
> Notice: Each update is specific, honest, and sets a next expectation. No vague 'Still waiting...'
>
> Regular communication prevents the client from imagining worst-case scenarios."

**[7:30-9:00] HANDLING DISAPPOINTMENT**

**VISUAL**: Real conversation scenario playing out on screen

**AUDIO - Presenter**:
> "Sometimes the insurer approves less than expected. The client is disappointed. How do you handle it?
>
> **CLIENT**: 'They only approved $7,000 of my $12,000 quote. That's ridiculous!'
>
> **YOUR RESPONSE** (professional, validating):
> 'I understand your frustration. The insurer approved $7,000, which covers the primary water damage. The additional $5,000 you quoted was for upgrades beyond water restoration. This is typical—insurers cover restoring to pre-loss condition, not improvements.'
>

> 'You have a few options: (1) Proceed with the $7,000 approved work, (2) Pay out-of-pocket for the additional upgrades, (3) Request that I contact the insurer about the exclusion, (4) You can file an AFCA complaint if you believe they've made an error.'
>
> This approach: Validates emotion + explains rationale + empowers the client with choices.
>
> The client goes from 'This is unfair' to 'These are my options.' That's professional.
>
> One more thing: Don't promise to 'fight the insurer' on their behalf. You can advocate by providing additional documentation, but ultimate coverage decisions are theirs. If they disagree, escalation to AFCA is their right."
